# Blueprint — connect the idea to the journey

## What it does

Nicolas Chan's Blueprint audits and designs connected journeys. Link tactics to the creative centre and audience progression. **Each arrow records what the CTA urges people to do next.** Plot our intended logical pathways; actual behaviour may differ. Ask “And then?” and “What are we asking them to do?”

## When to use it

Use inside Step 9 for campaign design, or audit existing journeys from their audience, objective and actual assets. Design retains the approved platform, hero and Strategy. Do not invent upstream decisions for an audit. Changed Insight or Strategy returns to its human checkpoint; media buying remains downstream.

## How to apply it

1. Choose stakeholder and audit view: ecosystem, campaign, isolated route or cross-team. Plot intended changes in thinking/action and routes through the encounters.
2. Place the hero, tactics and supporting assets. State each creative connection, audience barrier and message/proof. Give each a job: recruit, experience, amplify, enable, convert or sustain.
3. Label each arrow with the action the CTA urges: visit, buy, enquire, share or recommend. Name its destination or intended outcome and the message that makes the next step logical. Links, signs and points of sale help facilitate it; they are not prerequisites.
4. Show the ecosystem, then highlight one intended route. Distinguish channels, ownership and existing/proposed assets with a legend.
5. Audit missing support, orphan tactics, friction, overload and unclear intent. A gap needs a human reason, not an empty template cell or absent hyperlink.
6. Return a map, intended route and table: message/CTA → urged action → destination/outcome → facilitation. Note dependencies and fixes. People may take other routes; assess outcomes separately from adherence to the plan.

### Run it in practice

**Hold the creative centre fixed.** Write the platform rule, Big Idea and CTA above the canvas. Ask what each small idea does for that centre. It may introduce the hero, invite participation, let people experience it, extend its meaning, supply proof, enable action or sustain the relationship. A small idea need not send everyone through the hero first. Existing service pages, forms and CRM can support the route without becoming new creative ideas.

**Map the person before the media.** Who needs to change? Buyers, employees, partners and other stakeholders can need different routes. Use audience language such as “I discover,” “I compare,” “I enquire,” “I experience” or “I recommend.” Awareness → interest → consideration → conversion → experience → advocacy is a useful starting canvas, not a compulsory six-stage funnel. Choose only stages that explain this brief. Stage labels describe a mental or behavioural change, not a channel or campaign phase.

**Give each encounter a distinct job.** What question or barrier does it address? What message or proof advances the person? A headline may create curiosity; a demonstration may make the proposition believable; a landing page may remove uncertainty before action. Incremental messages retain the same creative rule while answering different questions. Repeating every key message at every touchpoint defeats the sequence.

**Make the intended action explicit.** Label the arrow with what we urge the person to do: search for the product, visit the website, RSVP, redeem a code, buy, share a post or recommend it to someone. Name the destination or intended outcome and explain how the message makes this next step logical. A hyperlink, sign, QR code, reminder or point of sale can facilitate that action. Where none exists, the message still expresses the intent. Distinguish audiences and alternative planned routes where useful. The arrows plot our intended pathways, not a prediction or record of everyone's actual behaviour. Record material asset or delivery dependencies where they matter.

**Read the canvas at three levels.** The ecosystem view shows coverage and missing assets. A highlighted route tests one audience's experience. A delivery and measurement overlay shows dependencies, ownership and contribution. Use distinct legend treatments for channel, ownership and status; a dashed outline can mark a proposed asset. Fading unused boxes prevents the ecosystem inventory from obscuring the route being tested.

**Diagnose before adding tactics.** Identify the exact break: no entry route, awareness-heavy inventory, unsupported comparison, unclear CTA, a missing destination, awkward sharing, no post-action experience, or an asset that supports no discernible journey. Show how a proposed service augments existing client assets. Do not manufacture work just to fill every column or include every channel.

### Audit views — choose the question before the canvas

The training deck presents several ways to audit the same ecosystem. Use the view that resolves the brief, and trace each route through a particular stakeholder's eyes.

| View | What to inspect | Training source |
|---|---|---|
| Stakeholder-specific journey | Who needs to change: business decision maker, buyer, employee, partner or another audience? Do groups need different entry points, messages and destinations? Adapt the number and meaning of stages. | Slides 23–24; new audiences versus returning contacts on slide 20 |
| Whole ecosystem / CMO audit | Inventory existing initiatives and assets. Is a necessary stage unsupported? Are tactics concentrated in one stage? Is the workload realistic for the team? | Slides 26–28 |
| Campaign / brand-manager audit | What happens when the person encounters this campaign? Check incremental messages, timing, the actions each CTA urges and whether the intended transitions make sense. | Slides 29–30, 36 |
| Isolated route audit | Highlight one path and fade the rest. Find an unsupported transition, meaningful friction or tactic with no plausible role in the journey. Allow indirect routes and useful detours. Compare alternative entry and reactivation routes. | Slides 20, 32, 55 |
| Cross-channel / cross-team audit | Trace traditional and digital encounters across the client, agency and event partner. Name the responsible owner at each handoff and how proposed services augment existing assets. | Slides 31–32 |

For multi-prong or multi-phase work, inspect each branch or phase and its handoffs; an overview alone does not establish an executable journey (slide 74). A missing step means an important audience need or transition lacks a credible supporting encounter, message or prompt. Do not treat an empty cell in a generic six-stage template as proof of a gap. Nor should fewer stages excuse a necessary transition that has disappeared from the drawing.

### The CTA intent principle

Nic's latest clarification on 10 October 2026 governs this integration: **the line shows what the call to action should be urging people to do. Intent matters.** This refines the earlier “intended pull” description and supersedes the literal direct-click requirement. Blueprint plots how we intend the consumer to make their way through a set of logical pathways we design.

For each arrow, state the intended action, its destination or outcome, and the message that urges it. “Visit the boutique,” “order here,” “go to the website” and “tell a friend” describe different intentions. Explain why the proposed next encounter makes sense in this journey. Merely drawing an arrow between two assets leaves the intent unclear.

Something that facilitates the action is best where appropriate: a hyperlink, a sign placed somewhere, a QR code or a point of sale that lets people order or buy. Where there is no such mechanism, the audience-facing message can still urge the intended action. An absent hyperlink alone does not make the conceptual connection broken. Check an actual mechanism's functionality when reviewing delivery, and mark material unknown assets or dependencies `[ TBC ]` where needed.

People inhabit a physical and social world. They can enter through different encounters, skip stages, return later or take a route we did not plot. Blueprint expresses our planning intent; it does not require the consumer to obey it or claim that our drawing predicts actual behaviour.

**Author's illustration:** an arrow from a post can mean “tell other people about this” because we intend word of mouth. Someone might instead visit the website and buy without telling a friend. That sale is still successful even though the person did not follow our intended journey. Preserve the planned word-of-mouth action on the map; record the actual purchase route separately when known. This illustration clarifies the method and is not a claim about the training campaigns.

Audit whether the intended actions and transitions are logical, whether important messages or experiences are missing, and whether available facilitation supports them. Fix disconnected tactics and broken handoffs without forcing every person through an exact sequence or manufacturing another touchpoint beyond a meaningful outcome. Plans, observed routes and measured results are distinct records.

### Required output

Supply one readable ecosystem map and at least one highlighted audience route. Mermaid, a slide, a canvas or an arrow diagram can serve; software is not the method. Put these records beside it:

| Touchpoint / idea | Link to platform and Big Idea | Audience / stage / barrier | Message or proof → action | Urged action / destination / facilitation | Owner / dependency | Evidence or KPI |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Named execution or supporting asset | What it expresses, proves or enables | Who arrives and what must change | What they encounter and what we ask them to do | Intended destination or outcome; mechanism where available | Who delivers it; what must exist first | What would show progression; observed or assumed |

Give each arrow a clear CTA intent and explain the logical transition. Flag unsupported progression, meaningful friction and disconnected tactics. Highlight our intended routes; they need not enumerate every path a person could take. Report the priority gaps and fixes, including dependencies still `[ TBC ]`. A compact path does not need every possible attribute repeated inside each box; the table holds the detail.

### Measurement overlay

Separate exposure, progression and the final behaviour. State both numerator and denominator for rates, with the source or assumption. If a route predicts visits or enquiries, show the arithmetic and what conversion means. Costs and conversion rates in a training example are illustrative estimates, not a benchmark or a causal result. Multiple touchpoints can contribute to one action; the map alone establishes no attribution.

## Failure mode

A wall of channel boxes with no audience progression; vague arrows; CTAs with no clear intended action or logical next encounter; treating planned routes as mandatory behaviour; unrelated executions sharing a slogan; the same message everywhere; speculative conversion presented as fact. Fix the broken connection before adding a tactic.

## Example

Nespresso's proposed #TakeYourTime route: partner invitation → Instagram hero → redemption code → boutique sampling → CRM. The invitation urges participation; the code urges a boutique visit for redemption.

### Nespresso — #TakeYourTime

The author's training deck, slides 64–68, presents a campaign proposal with a central Instagram activation: thirteen daily coffee moments lasting thirteen minutes, with a redemption code leading to boutique sampling. Slide 66 explicitly calls this the curtain raiser. Read it as the hero execution at Step 8; the surrounding routes show Step 9's work. The campaign year and realised outcomes are not established by this deck.

- **Recruit sponsors:** announcement coverage → sponsor pledge site → registration → toolkit. The sponsor sends employee email/calendar invitations, bringing another audience into the activation.
- **Recruit and engage consumers:** partner and influencer amplification → Instagram → notification/reminder → central activation → redemption code → boutique sampling.
- **Sustain the relationship:** sampling feeds CRM; the map proposes a later route to paid amplification for a product launch. This is a planned dependency and follow-up, not evidence of effectiveness.

The small ideas perform different jobs around one centre. The toolkit enables participation; invitations recruit; influencers amplify; the code connects the experience to sampling. They belong because of their specific contribution, not because they all carry #TakeYourTime. This Step 8/9 reading is the Logic Poetry integration of the author's diagram; it does not reconstruct an unrecorded Insight or GWTB.

### Shopline — incremental messages and an isolated route

Slide 36 changes the message as the audience moves from discovering the brand, to understanding business-model choices, to comparing suppliers. Slides 54–55 show the full ecosystem and then isolate the Innovation Summit journey: a whitepaper launch can prompt search, coverage provides a download route, product information leads to a trial, while summit participation can lead through a QR code into WeChat and further engagement. Faded assets keep the active route legible. The deck demonstrates sequencing and mapping; it does not establish conversion outcomes.

### HireRight — new audiences and existing contacts

Slide 20 separates traffic generation from reactivation of customers/cold leads. It connects paid encounters to owned content and an enquiry destination, includes direct conversion, and draws a return route for non-converters. This demonstrates branching and handoff design. It does not imply every person follows every stage.

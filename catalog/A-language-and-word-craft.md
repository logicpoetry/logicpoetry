### CLUSTER A: LANGUAGE & WORD CRAFT

Techniques that manipulate words, grammar, and linguistic structure to create memorability.

**A1. Word Mutation (Portmanteau)**
Blend two words into a new coinage that compresses an entire idea into one term. The audience solves a mini-puzzle to decode it, which creates a dopamine hit that aids recall.
- "Gymtimidation" — Planet Fitness (gym + intimidation)
- "FoodMarks" — GrowAsia (food + trademarks)
- "Brexit" — (Britain + exit)

**A2. Wrong-on-Purpose Grammar**
Deliberately break a grammatical rule to create a micro-jolt. The audience mentally "repairs" the error, which increases encoding and recall. Maps to "artful deviation" in rhetorical theory.
- "Think Different" — Apple (should be "Think Differently"; credited to Craig Tanimoto at TBWA\Chiat\Day)
- "got milk?" — California Milk Processor Board (credited to Jeff Goodby at Goodby Silverstein & Partners)

**A3. The Break-Up**
Split a word or phrase with a period, slash, or pause to create two simultaneous ideas from one expression.
- "Make.Believe" — Sony

**A4. The Noun-to-Verb Flip**
Turn a noun into a verb (or vice versa) to make the message active and urgent.
- "Spread the happy" — Nutella

**A5. Prefix Play**
Prepend "re-" (or another prefix) to a familiar word to suggest reinvention or transformation.
- "Banking Reimagined" — Capital One

**A6. Alliteration**
Repeat initial consonant sounds for rhythm and memorability.
- "Fumble, bumble, and tumble resistant" — Apple Watch

**A7. Tricolon / Rule of Three**
Group items in threes — the minimum number for the brain to recognize a pattern.
- "Oh. So. Pro." — Apple

**A8. The Pun**
Employ homophones or similar-sounding words to create humor and double meaning.
- "Shave Time. Shave Money." — Dollar Shave Club
- "Every kiss begins with Kay" — Kay Jewelers

**A9. The Pink Elephant**
Reference familiar phrases or musical hooks that audiences subconsciously complete. The brain encodes the brand by finishing the pattern.
- "I'm Lovin' It" — McDonald's (paired with a jingle audiences complete mentally)

**A10. The Read-Through**
Combine brand name and tagline to form a single flowing sentence.
- "De Beers… Diamonds Are Forever"

**A11. Elliptical Question Hook**
A question with missing context forces the audience to supply the scenario — a built-in engagement loop.
- "got milk?" — built from a "deprivation strategy" distilled into the shortest possible check-question.

**A11a. The Unfinished Thought**
Break off a sentence before it completes, leaving a deliberate gap the audience's brain automatically fills. The incompleteness creates a loop — the mind closes it, encoding the brand in the process.
- "You're Not You When You're..." — Snickers (the missing word is "Hungry"; the audience supplies it)
- "Make. Believe." — Sony (the pause between words forces the reader to hold both meanings simultaneously)
- Source: Classical rhetoric (Aposiopesis). The audience's act of completion is itself a form of engagement — you've made them participate in your message.

**A12. Rhetorical Question**
Pose a question whose answer reinforces the product's superiority.
- "What else?" — Nespresso

**A13. The Almost-Swear**
The missing letter/word forces the audience to supply the profanity. Controlled scandal with technical deniability.
- "FCK" — KFC (agency: Mother London)
- "Protect Like a Mother" — Lysol

**A14. Rhythm and Shine**
Phonic rhyming and symmetrical sentencing to create a "mental earworm" easy to encode and recall.
- "Chill if you spill." / "H2O. You're good to go." — Apple

**A15. Contrast / Antithesis**
Pair opposites next to each other to clarify a benefit with extreme precision.
- "Less to carry, more to love." — Apple
- "Built to last. Puts the earth first." — Apple

**A16. The Switchback**
Sets up a sentence where the second half delivers an unexpected turn that forces reinterpretation of the first half. The structure creates expectation, then violates it.
- "Good things come to those who wait... 119.5 seconds." — Guinness (cliché comfort + specific number = product proof)
- "Tastes Awful. And It Works." — Buckley's (honest negative makes the positive claim credible)
- Source: Classical rhetorical device; maps to Pricken's "Turn It Right Around" and Goldenberg's "Inversion."

**A17. The Mirror Line**
Reverses the grammatical structure in the second clause, creating a rhythmic, memorable, logically sharp expression. Forces the audience to hold two contrasting ideas simultaneously.
- "Stops static before static stops you" — Bounce
- "Ask not what your country can do for you — ask what you can do for your country" — JFK (studied in ad schools as a structural model)
- Source: Classical rhetoric (antimetabole); frequently taught at creative schools as a tagline construction technique.

**A18. Take It Literally**
Takes a figurative expression, cliché, or abstract concept and renders it literally and visually. The contrast between expected figurative meaning and literal execution creates surprise.
- "Red Bull Gives You Wings" — literally showing a person sprouting wings
- Source: Pricken's "Take It Literally" chapter. Staple exercise at Miami Ad School and VCU Brandcenter.

## RESEARCH_AND_CREATIVE_OPERATORS

An operator specifies what stays fixed, what changes and how to recognise a useful result. This library connects 26 research/design methods (R01–R26) and 15 word/sound operations (W01–W15) to the existing catalog. Overlaps are intentional cross-references, not claims of 41 independent inventions.

Use the menu to choose relevant operations. Read the full card when the procedure or source matters. Use creative-operator-routing.md for combinations and selection; research-brief.md works backwards from these input needs to a tailored research assignment.

### Shared evidence and execution rules

Keep original records unchanged. Transform interpretations, relationships, forms and potential actions. Every externally checkable claim must retain a detailed source citation with the complete visible URL immediately beside it; IDs are not substitutes. An analogy or transformed phrase does not validate a fact.

Public records can seed interview-style questions; they do not turn desk research into a participant study. Model-generated consumers, quotes and prototype reactions are hypotheses, not observations. Use lexicons or validators for exact constraints and real listeners for sound and recognition. These operations support creative exploration; they do not establish a probability of winning awards.

R and W IDs name operators; A–L IDs name existing craft entries. Select by mechanical difference and strategic fit. Do not force all operators into every brief or count overlapping aliases as different routes. Sources for the detailed procedures follow; the word tools are documented designs and observed interfaces from September 2026, not guaranteed current implementations.

## Detailed research and design cards

The procedures below are Logic Poetry applications of the attributed methods. Examples are illustrations unless a source or observation is explicitly identified. Placement notes link operations to the catalog and step guidance.

### A. Find material that ordinary desk research misses

#### R01. Critical Incident Technique — reconstruct the consequential moment

**Method.** John C. Flanagan's Critical Incident Technique collects specific behaviour in consequential situations. It directs attention towards what happened, rather than relying only on general opinions.

**Run.** Gather accounts of the last time somebody abandoned, improvised, complained, returned, recommended or unexpectedly succeeded. Reconstruct: situation → intention → action → obstacle → outcome. Preserve the person's wording separately from the researcher's interpretation.

**Look in:** detailed reviews, complaint narratives, community troubleshooting threads, diaries, existing interview transcripts and permitted customer-service records. Public anecdotes are leads and cases, not a representative sample.

**Produces:** a scene, a concrete friction and a testable question. Example: a hypothetical repair customer postpones booking whenever the form asks for a part number they cannot identify.

**Placement:** upstream research procedure for Steps 1–3; scenes can later inform Step 8.

**Check:** a dramatic complaint does not establish frequency. The LLM can extract and compare incidents; it cannot invent interviews or infer an unstated motive as fact.

**Source:** Flanagan, “The critical incident technique,” 1954, Psychological Bulletin 51(4), 327–358. APA's original-source record: https://www.apa.org/pubs/databases/psycinfo/critical.html. Procedural description checked through AHRQ's account of the paper: https://psnet.ahrq.gov/issue/critical-incident-technique. The original APA PDF did not render in this research session.

#### R02. Laddering / means–end chains — trace why a feature matters

**Method.** Reynolds and Gutman connect attributes, their consequences and personal values through in-depth questioning.

**Run.** Start with an observed choice. Ask what the feature enables, why that consequence matters, and when it stops mattering. Reconstruct several possible chains; retain only links supported by the respondent or evidence.

**Look in:** interviews, product comparisons, explanations of switching, reviews that explain trade-offs, and discussions of sacrifices people make.

**Produces:** competing motive chains for Step 3 and a capability-to-value bridge for Step 4. Hypothetical example: evening appointments → no need to leave work → less dependence on a manager's permission.

**Placement:** explicit research procedure supporting the existing insight excavation formula.

**Check:** repeatedly asking “why” can encourage rationalisation. Distinguish a participant's explanation from a demonstrated causal relationship. An LLM-generated ladder is a hypothesis until checked.

**Source:** Thomas J. Reynolds and Jonathan Gutman, “Laddering Theory, Method, Analysis, and Interpretation,” Journal of Advertising Research, February/March 1988, especially the laddering methods section. Article text inspected at https://studylib.net/doc/5870532/laddering-theory--method--analysis--and; university-hosted original PDF: https://is.muni.cz/el/econ/jaro2013/MPH_MVPS/39278324/LadderingTheoy_original.pdf.

#### R03. Lead-user research — find people already solving an emerging need

**Method.** Eric von Hippel identifies users whose needs run ahead of the wider market and who expect substantial benefit from finding a solution.

**Run.** Search for people who have modified the product, assembled workarounds, written unofficial instructions or built a substitute. Extract the need, the improvised mechanism and the cost they accepted.

**Look in:** maker communities, specialist forums, accessibility adaptations, open-source projects, enthusiast guides and professional edge cases.

**Produces:** emerging tensions, possible brand roles and demonstrable new behaviours. Example: users create their own repair-history labels before a brand provides any.

**Placement:** research procedure for Steps 1, 2 and 4; later a source for Step 8.

**Check:** an unusual user is not automatically a lead user. Establish both the forward-looking need and the incentive to solve it. Check whether the wider audience shares the need and can use the solution.

**Source:** Eric von Hippel, “Lead Users: A Source of Novel Product Concepts,” Management Science 32(7), 1986, 791–805; author's manuscript, abstract and identification criteria: https://evhippel.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/lead-users-paper-1986.pdf.

#### R04. Positive deviance — investigate success under the same constraints

**Method.** Positive deviance seeks uncommon, successful practices among people facing comparable problems and resources.

**Run.** Begin with a defined failure or obstacle. Find comparable cases that succeed. Investigate the actual behaviour responsible, then test whether others can adopt it.

**Look in:** practitioner accounts, community-led initiatives, unusually successful service locations, customer routines and documented local solutions.

**Produces:** an alternative explanation and a repeatable action. Example: one group completes repairs because it combines drop-off with an existing weekly journey.

**Placement:** new procedure supporting Steps 2–5, with potential participation mechanics for Steps 8–9.

**Check:** match constraints. Extra staff, money, access or unobserved advantages may explain the result. Positive deviance is not a label for any rebellious or surprising behaviour.

**Source:** Positive Deviance Collaborative, definitions of positive deviants and the positive deviance approach, https://positivedeviance.org/terms; its field guide, especially discovery and community-led practice, https://positivedeviance.org/s/FINALguide10072010.pdf.

#### R05. Negative-case and contrastive research — find where the attractive explanation fails

**Method basis.** Case research can challenge a general proposition through a case that contradicts it. This is a useful foundation for deliberate disconfirmation.

**Run.** For every promising claim, search its boundary: who does the opposite, under what conditions, and with what result? Compare similar contexts with different outcomes before proposing an explanation.

**Look in:** non-users, former customers, failed launches, dissenting reviews, abandonment cases and communities overlooked by the initial search.

**Produces:** a narrower, more credible insight—or a completely different tension. If “people want invisible repairs” fails among collectors, the next question becomes when visible history adds value.

**Placement:** explicit research procedure for Steps 1–3 and the Step 6 evidence check. It strengthens the existing belief-breaking and reframing work.

**Check:** finding one exception can defeat an absolute claim; it cannot establish how common the exception is. Preserve contradictory cases rather than explaining them away.

**Source:** Bent Flyvbjerg, “Five Misunderstandings About Case-Study Research,” Qualitative Inquiry 12(2), 2006, 219–245; discussion of falsification and case selection, author manuscript: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1304.1186.

#### R06. Cultural probes — invite unfamiliar ways of showing everyday life

**Method.** Gaver, Dunne and Pacenti used evocative tasks and materials to elicit responses that could inspire design.

**Run.** When direct questioning produces stock answers, use participant-created material: a map of avoided places, photographs of objects awaiting attention, an unfinished sentence, or a diary of a recurring ritual. Desk research can locate existing first-person material with similar qualities.

**Look in:** photo diaries, personal collections, community zines, autobiographical projects and, in an approved field study, participants' responses to purpose-designed tasks.

**Produces:** imagery, vocabulary, emotional associations and situations that conventional topic summaries miss.

**Placement:** research procedure for Step 1 and raw material for Step 7.

**Check:** treat interpretation as interpretation. Evocative responses do not establish prevalence, and an LLM impersonating participants does not constitute a probe study.

**Source:** Bill Gaver, Tony Dunne and Elena Pacenti, “Design: Cultural Probes,” interactions 6(1), 1999, 21–29, original article text: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220383091_Design_Cultural_probes. The authors' later methodological discussion is recorded at https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/4720/.

### B. Change the structure of the thought

#### R07. The semiotic square — explore more than two sides

**Method.** Greimas's semiotic square starts from a meaningful opposition, then distinguishes each term from its negation. Compound positions allow further exploration.

**Run.** Take a category opposition such as work/play. Explore work, play, non-work and non-play, then examine meaningful combinations. “Non-work” may include rest; it need not mean play. “Both” may suggest learning through play. Collect real cultural examples before choosing labels.

**Look in:** category language, social expectations, contrasting depictions, rituals and cases that do not fit the usual binary.

**Produces:** alternative cultural territories and possible reframes.

**Placement:** attributed method for Steps 1–3 and 7. It extends the existing interest in contradictions and opposing poles.

**Check:** these are semantic positions, not automatically real audience segments. Select an opposition meaningful to the audience. A negation is not proof that a coherent alternative exists.

**Source:** Louis Hébert, scholarly exposition of Greimas's model, “The Semiotic Square,” sections on terms, relationships and compound terms: https://www.signosemio.com/pages/greimas/semiotic-square.php. This is a methodological account, not empirical evidence of campaign effectiveness.

#### R08. Provocation plus movement — extract a useful principle from an impossible premise

**Method.** Edward de Bono's provocations include escape, reversal, exaggeration, distortion and wishful thinking. “Movement” develops something useful from the provocation.

**Run.** List an assumption; suspend or invert it; examine consequences; extract a useful principle; reconnect it to verified capability. Example: “The repair happens before the customer reports a problem” might lead to preventive checks triggered by known product age.

**Look in:** supposed necessities, category rules, process dependencies and recurring compromises.

**Produces:** a new research question, role hypothesis or execution mechanism.

**Placement:** enrich existing framework 11 and the Step 3/7 reframing guidance.

**Check:** “What if the opposite were true?” is incomplete. Require the bridge from provocative premise to useful mechanism. Keep the provocation labelled as fiction.

**Source:** Edward de Bono, “Serious Creativity,” sections on provocation and movement: https://www.debono.com/serious-creativity-article.

#### R09. Functional analogy — search for how something works elsewhere

**Method basis.** Function-oriented biomimicry asks what a system accomplishes and how it does so. The useful unit of transfer is the mechanism.

**Run.** Rewrite the problem as a verb: detect damage, distribute effort, signal trust, preserve history, coordinate strangers. Search nearby and distant domains for ways to perform that function. Map the mechanism, the necessary conditions and the mismatches.

**Look in:** AskNature, technical explanations, public-service systems, games, sports, logistics, maintenance and everyday institutions.

**Produces:** a mechanism card for a brand role or hero execution. Example: a repair service borrows the circulation logic of a lending library, rather than just its visual style.

**Placement:** enrich Step 7's analogy sweep; add explicit use at Steps 4 and 8.

**Check:** resemblance does not establish transferability. Verify biological claims and operational conditions. Search conceptual neighbours as well as distant sources.

**Sources:** Biomimicry Institute, “Seeing Function,” https://asknature.org/resource/seeing-function/; functional taxonomy, https://asknature.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Taxonomy_Explainer_2021.pdf. Chan, Dow and Schunn found benefits from nearer inspiration in their studied design setting; this cautions against equating distance with quality: https://joelchan.github.io/files/2014-desstudies-nearfar-in-press.pdf.

#### R10. Conceptual blending — combine organising structures

**Method.** Fauconnier and Turner describe selective projection from input spaces into a blend that can develop emergent structure.

**Run.** Give each source a structure: roles, rules, relationships and consequences. Select what transfers and what must change. Explore what becomes possible only in the combination.

**Look in:** systems with distinctive rules—passports, family trees, auctions, subscriptions, ceremonies, games and public records.

**Produces:** a new organising idea. Hypothetical example: repair history × passport creates an object whose accumulated interventions become part of its continuing identity.

**Placement:** a distinct procedure within the existing alchemy/association work at Step 7, with Step 8 applications. Use this shared procedure alongside the existing alchemy entry.

**Check:** two nouns placed together are only the starting point. Require a new rule or consequence and identify which source assumptions do not transfer.

**Source:** Gilles Fauconnier and Mark Turner, “Conceptual Integration Networks,” Cognitive Science 22(2), 1998, 133–187; input spaces, selective projection and emergent structure: https://markturner.org/cinLEA.pdf.

#### R11. General morphological analysis — combine dimensions with compatibility checks

**Method.** Tom Ritchey's account develops categorical dimensions and alternatives, then examines their consistency to reduce the possible configurations.

**Run.** At Step 7, hold the approved strategy fixed. Vary dimensions such as activated brand asset, meaning operation, experience form and participation rule. Check combinations for contradictions before developing a bounded selection.

**Look in:** the evidence library and verified capability inventory. Each value should be a substantive option, not a synonym.

**Produces:** traceable combinations with visible reasons for inclusion or rejection. Four dimensions with three values each create 81 possible configurations; they do not automatically create 81 useful ideas.

**Placement:** supporting generation guidance for Steps 7–9. Upstream exploration requires a separate matrix and the normal human checkpoints.

**Check:** pairwise compatibility does not prove that a whole concept works. Test the complete configuration. Do not vary approved strategic premises inside an execution exercise.

**Source:** Tom Ritchey, “General morphological analysis as a basic scientific modelling method,” Technological Forecasting & Social Change 126, 2018, 81–91: https://www.swemorph.com/pdf/tfsc-gma-basic.pdf.

### C. Extend the language and cultural-reference repertoire

#### R12. Anti-proverbs and counter-proverbs — alter familiar wisdom

**Method.** Anti-proverbs modify familiar sayings for effects including humour and criticism. Counter-proverbs can oppose the proposition itself.

**Run.** Record the original wording and its assumed lesson. Generate changes to the actor, consequence, value judgment or condition. Keep a separate lane for challenging the lesson rather than merely swapping a word.

**Look in:** proverb collections, regional sayings, public speeches, recurring advice, fables and documented variations.

**Produces:** cultural reframes and memorable expressions. A hypothetical “Good as older” reworks the familiar “good as new” value judgment; it still needs a relevant strategic foundation.

**Placement:** enrich B3, A9 and D4 at Step 7. A newly questioned assumption may return to Step 2 as a hypothesis.

**Check:** record familiarity by audience and language. Recognition and relevance both matter. A transformation can preserve an unwanted association from its source.

**Source:** Wolfgang Mieder, introduction to the Dictionary of Authentic American Proverbs, discussion of counter-proverbs and anti-proverbs, printed pp. 23–24: https://www.berghahnbooks.com/downloads/intros/MiederDictionary_intro.pdf.

#### R13. Snowclones — reuse a recognizable phrase structure

**Method.** A snowclone is a reusable, recognizable phrase pattern with replaceable elements.

**Run.** Save the structure and its fixed versus variable parts. Substitute material that changes the proposition while preserving enough of the pattern for recognition.

**Look in:** documented catchphrases, recurring headline constructions, memes, slogans and local conversational formulas.

**Produces:** families of titles or audience participation prompts.

**Placement:** enrich A9/D4 at Step 7 and, when the audience can complete the pattern meaningfully, Step 9.

**Check:** repeated use can make a construction feel stale. Determine which audience still recognizes it and whether the reference contributes anything beyond familiarity.

**Source:** Geoffrey K. Pullum, Language Log discussion of snowclones and clichés: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=3604. The campaign workflow above is the Logic Poetry application.

#### R14. Antanaclasis — repeat a word while changing its meaning

**Method.** The same word recurs with a different sense.

**Run.** Collect genuinely different meanings of strategically useful words. Build a sentence in which the shift carries the argument. A donation example is “Small change. Big change”: coins in the first phrase, a substantive difference in the second. This illustrates the construction without claiming originality.

**Look in:** dictionary senses, technical versus everyday usage, occupational language and local colloquialisms.

**Produces:** a compact relationship between functional and emotional meanings.

**Placement:** a more explicit subtype under A8 and the Step 7 word-craft guidance.

**Check:** both meanings must be natural in context. A strained second meaning reduces the effect. Dictionary and native-speaker checks are useful.

**Source:** Gideon O. Burton, Silva Rhetoricae, Brigham Young University, “Antanaclasis”: https://rhetoric.byu.edu/Figures/A/antanaclasis.htm.

#### R15. Zeugma — make one governing word connect different kinds of things

**Method.** A word, often a verb, governs multiple elements in a way that produces a meaningful shift.

**Run.** Pair a literal product action with an emotional or social consequence. Hypothetical seed: “Mend your jacket and your reputation.”

**Look in:** verbs that describe both physical action and human relationships; examples of the audience using those senses.

**Produces:** compressed benefit bridges, copy directions and visual briefs.

**Placement:** operation in A-language-and-word-craft at Step 7.

**Check:** the second object must matter to the actual strategy. Grammatical cleverness is not evidence that consumers experience the claimed consequence.

**Source:** Poetry Foundation glossary, “Zeugma”: https://www.poetryfoundation.org/education/glossary/Zeugma.

#### R16. Lipograms and semantic exclusion — make habitual wording unavailable

**Method.** A lipogram excludes one or more letters. A ban on category vocabulary is a related exercise used here, rather than a strict lipogram.

**Run.** For a formal pass, prohibit a specified letter. For a strategic expression pass, prohibit the five most predictable category words. Ask for concrete scenes and actions that express the idea under that constraint.

**Look in:** competitor headlines, repeated category phrases, clichéd metaphors and the brand's habitual vocabulary.

**Produces:** alternative expressions and images that ordinary synonym generation may miss.

**Placement:** enrich C2's constraint approach and Step 7 generation guidance.

**Check:** verify the literal constraint mechanically. After removing the constraint, judge whether the idea remains useful. Difficulty alone is not creative value.

**Source:** Oulipo, “Lipogramme”: https://www.oulipo.net/fr/contraintes/lipogramme. The category-word exclusion procedure is the Logic Poetry application.

#### R17. S+7 — use a reproducible dictionary substitution

**Method.** Jean Lescure's S+7 replaces each noun with the seventh following noun in a specified dictionary.

**Run.** Keep the original sentence and dictionary fixed; generate the transformed version; inspect it for an unexpected image or relationship. Translate promising discoveries into a separate creative hypothesis.

**Look in:** source-linked proverbs, category statements and approved strategy language, with a named dictionary edition or reproducible lexicon.

**Produces:** strange but traceable prompts for further exploration.

**Placement:** explicit constraint operation for Step 7.

**Check:** an LLM guessing a plausible replacement is not performing exact S+7. Use a lexicon tool for the formal operation. Never present mutated source text as an authentic quotation.

**Source:** Oulipo, “S+7,” Jean Lescure, 1961: https://oulipo.net/contraintes/s7.

### D. Make the thought tangible, behavioural and testable

The next three moves come from the five-tool SIT product/service tradition. The inspected catalog already includes subtraction and task unification. These provide additional explicit operations; they should be distinguished from the advertising-template summary currently in framework 06.

#### R18. SIT division

Split a component, process or sequence, then rearrange its parts.

**Run:** separate diagnosis, drop-off and collection around existing journeys. Research the actual sequence first.

**Placement:** catalog C-structure operation; Steps 8–9, constrained by Role and CTA.

**Output/check:** a service or format variation; verify dependencies and customer effort.

**Source:** Drew Boyd, Faculty Guide to SIT, pp. 4 and 17: https://drewboyd.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/DB-Faculty-Guide-Final.pdf.

#### R19. SIT multiplication

Copy a component and change a meaningful characteristic of the copy.

**Run:** give the repair receipt a second form that travels with the object and records its history.

**Placement:** catalog C-structure operation, Step 8.

**Output/check:** a useful additional function; duplication alone is insufficient.

**Source:** Drew Boyd, Faculty Guide to SIT, pp. 4 and 18: https://drewboyd.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/DB-Faculty-Guide-Final.pdf.

#### R20. SIT attribute dependency

Make a change in one attribute govern another.

**Run:** connect a service benefit to verified product age.

**Placement:** catalog C-structure operation, Steps 8–9.

**Output/check:** an activation rule; verify the data, economics and incentives.

**Source:** Drew Boyd, Faculty Guide to SIT, pp. 4 and 21: https://drewboyd.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/09/DB-Faculty-Guide-Final.pdf.

#### R21. COM-B — diagnose what is preventing the desired behaviour

**Method.** Michie, van Stralen and West organise behaviour around interacting capability, opportunity and motivation.

**Run.** For the proposed CTA, research whether people can do it, whether their environment makes it possible, and whether they want or remember to do it. Separate physical and psychological capability, physical and social opportunity, and reflective and automatic motivation.

**Look in:** actual task attempts, journey observations, access requirements, failed completions and participant explanations.

**Produces:** a barrier diagnosis before choosing an intervention. Example: wanting to repair does not solve the absence of a workable collection window.

**Placement:** explicit procedure in Steps 2 and 5; revisit at Steps 8–9.

**Check:** an LLM can propose competing diagnoses and evidence requests. It cannot determine which barrier dominates without relevant evidence.

**Source:** Susan Michie, Maartje M van Stralen and Robert West, “The behaviour change wheel,” Implementation Science 6, 42, 2011, especially Figure 1 and definitions: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1748-5908-6-42.

#### R22. EAST — vary the intervention around a diagnosed barrier

**Method.** The Behavioural Insights Team's EAST framework groups interventions under Easy, Attractive, Social and Timely.

**Run.** After the diagnosis, generate plausible changes to effort, salience, social context or timing. For each proposal, explain the barrier it addresses and the evidence needed to test that link.

**Look in:** forms, reminders, booking steps, real social practices, routine transitions and the moments when a decision becomes actionable.

**Produces:** alternative execution and amplification mechanics.

**Placement:** Step 5 defines the required change; Steps 8–9 choose and test concrete interventions.

**Check:** a framework label does not prove that an intervention will work. Use real social evidence and compare actual behaviour against a baseline.

**Source:** Behavioural Insights Team, EAST methodology, originally developed in 2014 and updated in 2024: https://www.bi.team/east-tool/methodology/.

#### R23. Design fiction — create an ordinary object from a changed world

**Method.** Julian Bleecker explores design fiction through objects and stories that make possible worlds concrete.

**Run.** Assume the selected brand role has become real. Create the everyday artifact that would exist: a receipt, instruction card, product label, public notice, certificate or service encounter. Work backwards to the capability needed to make it true.

**Look in:** institutional documents, interfaces, signage, ceremonies, operating procedures and emerging practices.

**Produces:** a tangible concept brief and a list of requirements. Example: an object-history label makes long ownership visible and prompts questions about verification, transfer and repair records.

**Placement:** a specific procedure for Step 8, connected to C1 and C14.

**Check:** label the artifact speculative. It is useful for discovery and discussion; it does not establish that the service exists or that audiences will accept it.

**Source:** Julian Bleecker, “Design Fiction: A Short Essay on Design, Science, Fact and Fiction,” March 2009, including discussion of prototypes embedded in stories: https://www.burg-halle.de/id-neuwerk/post-exit/wp-content/uploads/sites/55/2019/02/Bleecker-Julian-2009-Design-Fiction.-A-short-essay-on-design-science-fact-and-fiction.pdf.

#### R24. Service blueprinting — expose the support a public idea needs

**Method.** Bitner, Ostrom and Morgan describe service blueprinting as a way to visualise service processes and interdependencies around the customer experience.

**Run.** Map what the customer does and encounters, then the people, systems and handoffs required to deliver it. Search for a backstage capability that could become visible proof or useful participation.

**Look in:** service walkthroughs, operational documentation, staff accounts, customer journeys and ownership of each handoff.

**Produces:** a workable hero-execution mechanism, evidence opportunities and a list of operational gaps.

**Placement:** explicit supporting procedure for Steps 4, 8 and 9.

**Check:** “the brand could do this” needs an accountable capability owner. A compelling mockup cannot demonstrate delivery capacity.

**Source:** Mary Jo Bitner, Amy L. Ostrom and Felicia N. Morgan, “Service Blueprinting: A Practical Technique for Service Innovation,” California Management Review 50(3), 2008, 66–94; authors' institutional summary inspected at https://csl.wpcarey.asu.edu/service-blueprinting-a-practical-technique-for-service-innovation. The detailed mapping above is the Logic Poetry application.

#### R25. Experience prototyping — discover material by trying the experience

**Method.** Buchenau and Fulton Suri describe prototypes that let people experience existing or proposed conditions through active engagement.

**Run.** Stage the smallest meaningful version of a shortlisted execution: paper interactions, a role-played handoff, a temporary display or a manually operated service. Observe comprehension, confusion, effort and unexpected behaviour.

**Look in:** the proposed experience itself. This creates a new research source after the initial desk research.

**Produces:** revised interactions, better scenes, sharper language and feasibility evidence.

**Placement:** supporting procedure for Steps 8–9; findings can reopen earlier assumptions through the normal checkpoints.

**Check:** separate team rehearsal from audience evidence. Scripted LLM responses are scenario generation. People actually attempting the task supply observational evidence.

**Source:** Marion Buchenau and Jane Fulton Suri, “Experience Prototyping,” DIS 2000, pp. 424–433, abstract describing its three uses: https://courses.ischool.berkeley.edu/i262/f13/readings_pdf/p424-buchenau.pdf. The abstract was available through indexed text; full PDF retrieval was unsuccessful.

#### R26. Visual rhetoric matrix — vary both arrangement and intended meaning

**Method.** Phillips and McQuarrie distinguish three visual structures—juxtaposition, fusion and replacement—and three meaning operations: connection, comparison for similarity and comparison for opposition.

**Run.** Keep one strategic relationship fixed, then sketch several arrangements and meaning operations. Explain the intended inference before generating finished imagery.

**Look in:** visual pairs with a demonstrable relationship, material properties, recognizable symbols and the brand's own assets.

**Produces:** different visual arguments and hero-image briefs.

**Placement:** enrich framework 12 and catalog G at Steps 7–8. The meaning dimension is the main addition to the current seed entry.

**Check:** test the visual without its explanatory paragraph. An ingenious interpretation that viewers do not make is a weak execution.

**Source:** Barbara J. Phillips and Edward F. McQuarrie, “Beyond Visual Metaphor: A New Typology of Visual Rhetoric in Advertising,” Marketing Theory 4(1/2), 2004, 113–136, Figure 2 and explanation: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/235361038_Beyond_Visual_Metaphor_A_New_Typology_of_Visual_Rhetoric_in_Advertising.

#### W01. Cross-domain analogy — Simile

**Reconstruction:** Turn an idea into a concrete comparison by moving it into another domain while retaining a useful shared property or relationship.

- **Start with:** A concept, quality or experience.
- **Keep:** The particular quality you want the listener to understand.
- **Change:** The object, setting or domain used to picture it.
- **Procedure:** Identify a property → find a concrete thing that exhibits it → state the comparison → check whether the image communicates that property without a long explanation.
- **Illustration:** A deadline is like a closing train door. The shared relationship is an approaching loss of opportunity.
- **Check:** A surprising image still needs a legible connection. A merely decorative comparison can leave the original idea unexplained.

**Evidence:** The documented prompt asks for concrete, unexpected, evocative comparisons. Locator: `SIMILE_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, beginning at line 48. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

#### W02. Sound-alike reinterpretation — Explode

**Reconstruction:** Hear a word as a different sequence of words. Shift boundaries, spelling or near-homophones so a familiar sound carries another meaning.

- **Start with:** A word or short phrase.
- **Keep:** Approximately the spoken sound.
- **Change:** Word boundaries, spelling and the resulting interpretation.
- **Procedure:** Say it aloud → try alternative syllable boundaries → replace segments with similarly sounding words → assemble a second readable meaning.
- **Observed:** `alone → a loan` appeared in a live run. The same run also returned unchanged or weakly transformed candidates, demonstrating that successful generation does not guarantee a valid puzzle solution. Live tool: https://textfx.withgoogle.com/
- **Check:** Read both versions aloud in the intended accent. Confirm that the new version supplies a genuinely different reading; punctuation alone is insufficient.

**Evidence:** The prompt's examples use same-sounding words and phrases. Locator: `EXPLODE_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 115. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

#### W03. Expectation violation — Unexpect

**Reconstruction:** Start with an ordinary scene, then disrupt an assumption that makes it ordinary.

- **Start with:** An action or situation.
- **Keep:** Enough of the original scene for the change to register.
- **Change:** An actor, material, place, rule, purpose, timing or other expected detail.
- **Procedure:** Describe the expected version → identify its assumptions → alter one or more → examine the consequences. These categories are my decomposition of the operation; the tool does not expose a formal menu of them.
- **Observed:** With a bus-waiting scene, one output replaced the driver with pigeons; another restricted time travel to Tuesdays. Live tool: https://textfx.withgoogle.com/
- **Check:** Identify exactly which expectation was broken. Random strangeness can be vivid while providing little further meaning.

**Evidence:** The source demonstrates added details producing unexpected twists; it does not enforce a single changed detail. Locator: `UNEXPECT_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 261. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

#### W04. Associative stepping — Chain

**Reconstruction:** Move through successive associations, using each arrival as the next departure point.

- **Start with:** One seed word.
- **Keep:** A defensible connection between each adjacent pair.
- **Change:** The current word, and potentially its sense or domain, at every step.
- **Procedure:** Seed → related word → word related to that word → repeat. Collocations and words with several meanings allow turns into new territory.
- **Illustration:** `light → feather → bird → nest → home → key → lock → secret`.
- **Check:** Explain every link. The final word can be distant from the seed while the journey remains intelligible.

**Logic Poetry application extension — branch and reconnect:**

When using Chain, explore branches as well as a single sequence. Seeds can include professional language, behaviours, objects, cultural references, stereotypes or a brand benefit. Distinguish observed facts from cultural associations and assumptions.

At words with several meanings, follow the different meanings separately. Look for words, phrases or experiences reached from more than one path. Explain what each path contributes. Meeting at the same word is useful only when the connections add relevant meaning; several paths do not independently verify an audience claim.

Keep the useful intermediate links visible. Do not discard an obvious seed before exploring what it can lead to. Stop or change direction when the links require strained explanations.

**Evidence:** The documented prompt specifies eight words; its filter checks the starting word, length and repeated items. Eight is an implementation choice, not a necessary property of the general technique. Locators: `CHAIN_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 302, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js ; `processChainOutputs`, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/postprocess.js

#### W05. Interpretive reframing — POV, historical

**Reconstruction:** Hold the subject steady and generate different judgments or interpretations of it.

- **Start with:** A topic.
- **Keep:** The topic being considered.
- **Change:** The evaluative stance, metaphor, emphasis or interpretation.
- **Procedure:** Generate several readings of the same thing → identify what each reading values or assumes → compare how the topic changes under those readings.
- **Illustration:** A queue can represent fairness, wasted time, or visible demand. These are possible interpretations, not findings about an audience.
- **Check:** Different wording does not establish a different viewpoint. Each output should reveal another reading or assumption.

**Evidence:** The archived prompt generates provocative or thought-provoking takes. It does not require stakeholder roleplay or paired opposites. Locator: `POV_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 358. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

**Availability:** Documented in the original source; absent from the live menu observed on 16 September 2026. https://textfx.withgoogle.com/

#### W06. Topic-and-initial intersection — Alliteration

**Reconstruction:** Build a vocabulary pool that satisfies both a meaning constraint and an opening-letter constraint.

- **Start with:** A topic plus a chosen initial letter or letter group.
- **Keep:** Topical relevance and the requested beginning.
- **Change:** The candidate word.
- **Procedure:** Expand the topic into actions, objects, qualities and terminology → retrieve candidates with the required beginning → check their actual pronunciation.
- **Illustration:** Cooking + B → bake, boil, braise.
- **Check:** Matching letters need not produce matching sounds. Also reject words whose connection to the topic is too weak to be useful.

**Evidence:** The prompt explicitly combines topic and initial letters. The historical implementation mainly filters spelling and includes special treatment for F/PH; it is not a complete phonetic validator. Locators: `ALLITERATION_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 411, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js ; `processAlliterationOutputs`, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/postprocess.js

#### W07. Backronym construction — Acronym

**Reconstruction:** Begin with an existing word and invent a phrase whose initials reproduce that word in order. This direction of construction is commonly called a backronym.

- **Start with:** A word that will become the initials.
- **Keep:** Its ordered letters.
- **Change:** The words and meaning of the expansion.
- **Procedure:** Assign a word to each initial → arrange the expansion into a coherent phrase → revise for naturalness → verify the initials.
- **Illustration:** CARE → Check And Repair Early.
- **Check:** The phrase must make sense as language. Perfect initials can conceal an incoherent or heavily padded expansion.

**Evidence:** The source starts from the letters of the input word, then checks the initials of generated expansions. Locators: `ACRONYM_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 464, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js ; `processAcronymOutputs`, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/postprocess.js

#### W08. Shared-property discovery — Fuse

**Reconstruction:** Find a surprising property or relationship that two apparently distant things have in common.

- **Start with:** Two concepts.
- **Keep:** Both concepts.
- **Change:** The dimension along which they are compared.
- **Procedure:** Describe each thing's functions, structures, behaviours and effects → look for a correspondence → explain why it fits both.
- **Observed:** A library/garden run found connections around cultivation, arrangement and care. This is a paraphrase of sampled output. Live tool: https://textfx.withgoogle.com/
- **Check:** The connection should be specific enough to reveal something. “Both are interesting” provides almost no discrimination.

**Evidence:** The source explicitly searches for a commonality between the two inputs. Locator: `FUSE_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 575. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

#### W09. Sensory decomposition — Scene

**Reconstruction:** Expand a scene into concrete cues that a person could see, hear, touch, smell or taste.

- **Start with:** A setting, event or situation.
- **Keep:** The scene as the organising context.
- **Change:** The level of description, from a label to particular sensory details.
- **Procedure:** Walk through the senses → generate specific objects, sensations and traces → select details that evoke the scene.
- **Illustration:** A late bus stop becomes a wet bench, fogged glasses and a diesel rumble.
- **Check:** A detail should evoke something particular. Generated details remain imagined material until checked against an actual place or experience.

**Evidence:** The documented prompt names the five senses and asks for concrete, evocative detail. Locator: `SCENE_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 632. https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js

#### W10. Existing-context harvesting — Unfold

**Reconstruction:** Find the words, compounds and familiar phrases in which a seed already occurs, exposing its different contexts and senses.

- **Start with:** A target word.
- **Keep:** Its occurrence within a larger expression.
- **Change:** The surrounding expression and the sense the word takes within it.
- **Procedure:** Search compounds, idioms, common expressions and recognisable titles → collect different contexts → distinguish established expressions from invented ones.
- **Observed:** A `light` run included `green light`, `lighthearted` and `shed light on`. Live tool: https://textfx.withgoogle.com/
- **Check:** Establish that the expression really exists before treating it as culturally familiar. The historical filter only checks whether the seed occurs in the output; that does not verify familiarity or provenance.

**Logic Poetry application extension — useful overlapping meanings:**

After finding established expressions containing a seed, compare their different meanings. When a phrase connects naturally to the audience, the brand's intended benefit or a possible experience, develop that connection.

Look for expressions whose meanings reinforce one another. More meanings are not automatically better: each should add something useful. Keep established phrases separate from invented variations, and check familiarity where recognition matters.

**Evidence:** Locator: `UNFOLD_PROMPT_COMPONENTS`, line 703, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/priming.js ; `processUnfoldOutputs`, https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/generative-ai-docs/main/demos/palm/web/textfx/src/lib/postprocess.js

### Nonsense Laboratory

#### W11. Phonetic blending — Mixer

**Reconstruction:** Combine the sound patterns of input words and turn the mixture into a new spelling.

- **Start with:** Two or more words.
- **Keep:** Some sound material from the inputs; preservation is approximate.
- **Change:** How that material combines and is spelled.
- **Procedure:** Consider the words as sound shapes → blend those shapes → produce a pronounceable candidate → listen for what survives.
- **Observed:** The default inputs `ketchup + mustard` produced `kestured`. The help permits up to six inputs and suggests two or three work best.
- **Check:** Listen for the parent words and assess pronounceability. A blended sound does not automatically convey a blended meaning.

**Evidence:** Live Mixer and its Help. https://artsexperiments.withgoogle.com/nonsense-laboratory/#/mixer

Pincelate documents blending internal sound representations. This supports the general mechanism; it does not establish the precise weighting used by this web interface. https://github.com/aparrish/pincelate

#### W12. Sound-preserving spelling constraint — Respeller

**Reconstruction:** Re-encode a word's approximate pronunciation while restricting the letters available to write it.

- **Start with:** A word or phrase and an allowed alphabet.
- **Keep:** Approximately the spoken form, plus compliance with the letter restriction.
- **Change:** Its written form.
- **Procedure:** Sound out the input → remove prohibited letters from the available spelling choices → find substitute spellings → read the result aloud.
- **Observed:** With A/E/I/O/U excluded and Y available, `bright light → bryt lyt`.
- **Check:** Verify the allowed-letter rule separately from how recognisable the pronunciation remains. The two constraints can pull against each other.

**Evidence:** Live Respeller controls, Help and sampled output. https://artsexperiments.withgoogle.com/nonsense-laboratory/#/respeller

#### W13. Phonetic feature adjustment — Mouthfeel Tuner

**Reconstruction:** Alter how a phrase feels to articulate by changing selected sound qualities.

- **Start with:** A word or phrase.
- **Keep:** An adjustable degree of resemblance to the input.
- **Change:** Vowel qualities and consonant features, through controls for such qualities as hiss, breathiness, hardness and nasality.
- **Procedure:** Choose a sound feature → increase or reduce its influence → respell the altered sounds → read aloud and compare.
- **Observed:** `bright light → brighty lighty` with the EEE control at 0.75 and weirdness at 0.3. This is one captured result, not a universal transformation rule.
- **Check:** Judge the sound, the ease of saying it and the surviving resemblance separately. The control names do not establish universal emotional effects.

**Evidence:** Live Mouthfeel interface, Help and sampled output. https://artsexperiments.withgoogle.com/nonsense-laboratory/#/mouthfeel

#### W14. Articulatory sequencing — Sequencer

**Reconstruction:** Compose a sequence of mouth movements, then ask for a spelling that expresses it.

- **Start with:** A pattern of sound features across time steps.
- **Keep:** The intended sequence as the input specification.
- **Change:** The resulting word or spelling.
- **Procedure:** Choose consonant and vowel features in sequence → combine them across the grid → generate a word → compare the spoken result with the intended pattern.
- **Observed:** The interface provides eight time steps and controls including nasal, voiced, bilabial and velar. Loading the `mumble` preset and pressing Play produced `mumble`.
- **Check:** A preset demonstrates the feature-to-spelling path. It does not establish the quality of arbitrary invented combinations or precise reproduction of every selected feature.

**Evidence:** Live Sequencer grid, Help and preset test. https://artsexperiments.withgoogle.com/nonsense-laboratory/#/sequencer

#### W15. Sound-neighbourhood exploration — Explorer

**Reconstruction:** Browse small changes through a landscape organised by sound similarity.

- **Start with:** A position on the word map, a searched real word, or a random jump.
- **Keep:** Local sound resemblance between nearby positions.
- **Change:** The word gradually as you move through the field.
- **Procedure:** Locate a word → read nearby variants aloud → follow a row, column or diagonal → jump elsewhere to explore another region.
- **Observed:** The visible field placed the real word `intolerance` among invented neighbours. Help explicitly explains sound-based proximity and says the grid wraps around at its edges.
- **Check:** Proximity is phonetic; a nearby invented word need not share meaning or be recognised by a reader. The exact distance calculation was not established by this inspection.

**Evidence:** Live Explorer field and Help. https://artsexperiments.withgoogle.com/nonsense-laboratory/#/explorer
